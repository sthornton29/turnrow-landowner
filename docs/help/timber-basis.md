## What basis is, in one line

Your **basis** in timber is what it cost you (or what it was worth when you inherited it). When timber sells, you are taxed on the proceeds minus selling costs minus the basis of the timber that was cut, called **depletion**. With no basis on record, the IRS treats it as zero and the whole check is taxable. Everything here is for your accountant: confirm with them before filing.

## Setting up a tract (once)

Timber, then **Timber basis**, lists every property with timber stands. **Set up timber basis** asks:

- **Who owns the timber**: the entity that files the taxes (defaults to the property's entity).
- **How it came to them**: bought it, inherited it, received it as a gift, put into the LLC or trust by the family, or not sure. Each choice explains what sets the basis: for a purchase, the timber's share of the price plus closing costs; for an inheritance, the value on the date of death (stepped up); for a gift, the giver's basis carries over.
- **When**, and **what the whole property was worth** then.
- **How much was timber**: the timber, bare land, and improvements split from the closing statement or appraisal. If you have appraised values that do not add up to the price, enter them and use **Split the price by these values**: the price is divided in proportion (the USDA example: a $33,000 purchase with land appraised at $10,000 and timber at $20,000 gives a $22,000 timber basis).
- **Where the number comes from**: closing statement, cruise, appraisal, estate appraisal, retroactive appraisal, or your own estimate.
- **Timber by product (optional)**: if a cruise counted the timber, enter each product (pine sawtimber, pulpwood, ...) with its volume and its share of the value. Without volumes, one "All timber" line is fine.

No record of the value? That is common. A consulting forester can do a **retroactive appraisal** that works the timber back to the date you got it.

## At a sale

A tract's page lists every timber sale on its stands with what this tract received (its share, when a sale covers more than one property), the selling costs recorded on its timber work, the depletion, and the approximate taxable gain.

- **Pay-as-cut with volumes**: the settlements say how many tons of each product were cut, so Turnrow proposes depletion as tons times the basis per ton (USDA: 500 tons of sawtimber at $10 and 3,000 tons of pulpwood at $1 recover $8,000). **Use this** records it.
- **Lump sum, or no volumes on record**: answer "About how much of this tract's timber was sold?" (a clearcut of the whole tract is 100%), and Turnrow proposes that share of the basis.
- **Remove and redo** takes a sale's depletion back out.

Depletion never goes past the basis or volume on record; if more was cut than the account shows, it says so.

## The ledger

Every change to basis is a line: depletion from a sale, a cost added to basis, a volume adjustment (a new cruise, growth since you acquired it), a casualty loss (storm, fire, beetles: the loss is limited to your basis), or another adjustment. **Basis left** and the basis per ton are worked out from the lines; nothing is overwritten.

## Evidence

Attach the closing statement, estate appraisal, or cruise to the tract under **Evidence**. Keep them as long as you own the land plus the years a return can be examined.

## Common questions

- **Do I need Form T?** Usually when you claim depletion, though occasional sellers (a sale or two every few years) may not. Ask your accountant.
- **The per-ton number looks wrong.** It is the basis left divided by the volume on record. A new cruise posted as a volume adjustment corrects it.
- **Two entities own parts of one property.** Set up one account per owner and split the acres and values between them.
